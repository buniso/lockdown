Data Critique

What our dataset can tell us, what it can't, and what it means that happiness has been organized this way in the first place.

Xinyi Zhang — how the data was generated

The World Happiness Report ranks 156 countries by their citizens' happiness without directly measuring happiness itself. Instead, it combines survey responses with information from public databases to estimate levels of well-being. Additional data, including GDP per capita, healthy life expectancy, and health spending, are drawn from government reports and international organizations. Researchers combine these measures into a single dataset that allows countries to be compared using the same indicators.

Jasmine Gonzalez — what the original sources are

People from different countries are asked to rate how satisfied they are with their lives using the Cantril Ladder, a scale from 0 (the worst possible life) to 10 (the best possible life). Gallup collects these responses through face-to-face interviews or telephone surveys, typically sampling 1,000-2,000 people per country. The surveys also gather information on social support, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption.

Understanding how the dataset was created is important for evaluating both its strengths and its limitations. The core source is the Gallup World Poll, which has surveyed people in more than 150 countries using a standardized set of questions since 2005. Additional variables, including GDP per capita and healthy life expectancy, are drawn from organizations such as the World Bank, the World Health Organization, and the OECD. These indicators are then incorporated into regression analyses to estimate how strongly each factor is associated with differences in countries' reported happiness scores. The 2020 report reflects data averaged from 2017-2019 and was compiled by researchers at the Sustainable Development Solutions Network and Columbia University's Center for Sustainable Development using Gallup's survey data.

Kayla Camejo — who funded the dataset

The World Happiness Report is published annually by the Wellbeing Research Centre at the University of Oxford, an interdisciplinary research group focused on the study of human well-being. It is produced in collaboration with Gallup, a global analytics and advisory firm founded in 1935, and the United Nations Sustainable Development Solutions Network (SDSN). The Gallup World Poll is funded through a combination of Gallup's own research initiatives, contracts with partner organizations, and commissioned projects. For the World Happiness Report specifically, Gallup collects and licenses the survey data to the report's researchers. While Gallup has extensive experience conducting international surveys, its role as a private analytics company also highlights that the production of global datasets depends on organizations with the financial and institutional resources to conduct large-scale polling.

The report also draws on contributions from academic partners across North America, Europe, and East Asia, including the University of British Columbia's Vancouver School of Economics, Columbia University, Simon Fraser University, Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, the International Society for Quality-of-Life Studies, and the Centre for Economic Performance. It is important to acknowledge the possible biases that may arise when research leadership is concentrated within institutions in the Global North while the dataset represents more than 150 countries around the world. Decisions about how happiness is defined, measured, and interpreted may not fully reflect the perspectives of the populations being studied.

Yihan Liu — what the dataset can illuminate

One of the dataset's greatest strengths is its consistency. Since the same survey methodology has been administered across countries and over time, researchers can compare well-being both horizontally across nations and vertically across years. This consistency makes it possible to identify relationships between happiness and factors such as income, health, social support, and governance while also providing policymakers with evidence for understanding how major events may influence people's evaluations of their lives. The 2020 edition is particularly valuable because it captures global well-being before the COVID-19 pandemic, providing a useful baseline for later comparisons.

Jae Vin Lee — what information is left out

However, the dataset cannot capture the full complexity of human well-being. By relying on national averages, it obscures the diverse experiences of people living within the same country. Happiness can vary substantially according to income, race, ethnicity, gender, age, disability, religion, immigration status, sexuality, and geographic location. A country may rank highly overall while many communities continue to experience poverty, discrimination, violence, housing insecurity, or limited access to healthcare.

The dataset also leaves out many of the social, historical, and cultural factors that shape well-being. It cannot fully account for family relationships, community belonging, colonial histories, war, political repression, racism, labor conditions, housing affordability, climate change, or environmental injustice. Likewise, some populations may be underrepresented in the survey because of homelessness, incarceration, displacement, language barriers, limited access to telephones or the internet, or political instability.

An additional limitation is that the concept of "happiness" itself may not be understood in the same way across cultures. Although Gallup uses carefully translated questionnaires and standardized survey procedures, concepts such as happiness, life satisfaction, and the best possible life do not necessarily carry identical meanings across languages and cultural contexts. Cultural values influence how individuals evaluate and express well-being. For example, some cultures emphasize humility and discourage people from rating their lives too highly, while others place greater value on individual achievement or optimism. As a result, respondents may interpret and answer the same survey question differently, even when their lived experiences are similar.

Happiness is also inherently subjective and context dependent. A person's evaluation of their life may change depending on recent experiences, relationships, financial circumstances, physical health, or even their mood on the day they complete the survey. Although averaging thousands of responses helps reduce individual fluctuations, the resulting score should be understood as a snapshot of subjective well-being rather than a fixed or objective measure of happiness.

Dan Pham & Vaishavi Kagita — the ideology of the dataset's ontology, and what would be missing if this were the only source

These limitations are connected to how the dataset is structured. By organizing happiness into measurable categories – including economic conditions, institutional quality, health, and individual perceptions – the World Happiness Report presents well-being as something that can be quantified, compared, and partially explained through statistical indicators. This approach privileges forms of knowledge that can be measured while making other dimensions of well-being less visible. If this dataset were the only source available, much of the cultural, historical, and social context behind the numbers would be lost because aspects such as spirituality, cultural values, identity, and community belonging are difficult to standardize across countries.

Dan Pham — the ideology of the dataset's ontology, continued

The organization of the spreadsheet reinforces this perspective. The first columns present each country's rank and overall happiness score, followed by explanatory variables such as GDP per capita, healthy life expectancy, social support, freedom, generosity, and perceptions of corruption. This arrangement encourages readers to understand happiness primarily through measurable indicators while comparing countries according to a common numerical standard. By reducing entire nations to a single score and ranking, the dataset obscures inequalities within countries and minimizes differences among regions and communities. The prominence of the ranking columns is especially significant because it frames happiness as something countries compete over rather than as a complex and deeply contextual human experience.

In this way, the ontology of the dataset presents happiness as a universal, measurable, and comparable phenomenon. While this standardization enables meaningful international comparisons, it also assumes that people across cultures interpret and express well-being in sufficiently similar ways. These methodological choices shape not only what becomes visible in the data but also how readers come to understand happiness itself.
