Data Critique

What our dataset can tell us, what it can't, and what it means that happiness has been organized this way in the first place.

Xinyi Zhang — how the data was generated

The data was generated by combining survey responses with information from public databases. People from different countries were asked to rate how satisfied they were with their lives through large-scale surveys. Other information, such as GDP per capita, healthy life expectancy, social support, and health spending, was collected from government reports and international organizations. Researchers combined all of this into one dataset organized so that countries could be compared using the same measures. Although the dataset is based on reliable sources, the results still depend on how the surveys were conducted, how people answered the questions, and how each country collected and reported its statistics.

Jasmine Gonzalez — what the original sources are

The core source is the Gallup World Poll, which since 2005 has surveyed people in over 150 countries using the same set of questions every time, including the main "life evaluation" question — the Cantril Ladder, from 0 (worst possible life) to 10 (best possible life). Gallup collects this through face-to-face interviews or landline phone surveys, sampling roughly 1,000–2,000 people per country.

The six explanatory factors layered on top — GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption — are also pulled from Gallup's survey questions, supplemented by outside sources like the World Bank for GDP figures and WHO-adjusted life expectancy data. They're used afterward through regression analysis to estimate how much each one correlates with why some countries score higher than others. The 2020 report reflects data averaged from 2017–2019 and was compiled by researchers at the Sustainable Development Solutions Network and Columbia University's Center for Sustainable Development, based on the original Gallup data going back to 2005.

Kayla Camejo — who funded the dataset

The World Happiness Report is published annually by the Wellbeing Research Centre at the University of Oxford, founded in September 2019, working alongside Gallup (a global analytics and advisory firm since 1935) and the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network (SDSN), which operates under the UN Secretary-General to support the UN Sustainable Development Goals. Academic partners include the University of British Columbia's Vancouver School of Economics, Columbia University's Center for Sustainable Development, Simon Fraser University, Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, the International Society for Quality-of-Life Studies, and the Centre for Economic Performance. Corporate sponsors include Ajinomoto, Bloom Wellbeing Fund, Blue Zones, Fondazione Ernesto Illy, Glico, and Illy.

It is worth noting that many of these institutions are European, American, or East Asian — worth flagging as a possible source of bias in the collection of data across more than 140 countries without direct representation from many of them in the research process.

Yihan Liu — what the dataset can illuminate

Because the survey was conducted the same way across all included countries, the dataset allows direct comparison of people's own life evaluations against official metrics. Since Gallup has run the World Poll since 2005, the dataset can also track well-being over time — both the horizontal comparison across countries and the vertical comparison across years can give policymakers hints about how events connect to how people respond. The 2020 edition is especially useful as a "happiness" baseline captured just before the COVID-19 pandemic.

Jae Vin Lee — what information is left out

The spreadsheet leaves out the different experiences people may have within the same country, since it summarizes each country with average scores. Happiness can vary greatly by income, race, ethnicity, gender, age, disability, religion, immigration status, sexuality, or area of residence — a country may rank highly overall while some communities suffer poverty, discrimination, violence, unsafe housing, or limited healthcare access.

The spreadsheet also cannot represent many social and historical factors: personal stories, local context, family relationships, community belonging, colonial history, war, political repression, racism, labor conditions, housing costs, climate change, or environmental injustice. Some people may also be missing from the surveys entirely due to homelessness, incarceration, displacement, language barriers, limited internet or phone access, or political instability.

Dan Pham — the ideology of the dataset's ontology

Building on what is omitted, it's worth considering the ideological effects of how the dataset is organized. The World Happiness Report breaks happiness down into measurable factors — GDP per capita, life expectancy, social support, freedom, generosity — treating well-being as something that can be quantified, compared, and explained through data.

This structure prioritizes what can be measured while making other dimensions of happiness less visible. Cultural identity, spirituality, purpose, family obligations, and community belonging are largely absent because they resist standardized metrics. The dataset also reduces entire countries to a single score, which can obscure inequalities within countries and reinforce the idea that a national average can represent millions of diverse experiences.

Finally, the emphasis on rankings shapes how readers interpret the data. By ranking countries from happiest to least happy, the dataset frames happiness as something countries compete over — the focus shifts from understanding what helps people flourish to determining which countries perform better than others. In this way, the ontology of the dataset presents happiness as a universal, measurable phenomenon, shaping not only what we know about happiness but how we think about it.

Vaishavi Kagita — if this dataset were the only source

If this dataset were the only source, a lot of the bigger picture behind the numbers would be missing. The data shows how happy people are on average in each country based on factors like GDP, health, and social support — but not why they are that happy. It doesn't account for personal experience, cultural diversity, major historical events, political situations, or other local factors that shape well-being. Because it only shows country averages, it also hides differences between people living in the same country: some groups or regions may have much lower or higher levels of happiness, and those differences don't show up. On its own, this dataset wouldn't tell us about current events, political policies, or social matters that may have influenced people's responses — the data is incomplete, and we need other sources for a fuller perspective.
