Data Critique

What our dataset can tell us, what it can't, and what it means that happiness has been organized this way in the first place.

Xinyi Zhang — how the data was generated

The World Happiness Report ranks 156 countries by their citizens' happiness without directly measuring it. Instead, it combines survey responses with information from public databases to estimate levels of well-being. Additional data, including GDP per capita, healthy life expectancy, and health spending, are drawn from government reports and international organizations. Researchers combine these measures into a single dataset that allows countries to be compared using the same indicators.

Jasmine Gonzalez — what the original sources are

People from different countries are asked to rate how satisfied they are with their lives using the Cantril Ladder, a scale from 0 (worst possible life) to 10 (best possible life). Gallup collects these responses through face-to-face interviews or telephone surveys, typically sampling 1,000–2,000 people per country. The surveys also gather information on social support, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption.

Understanding how the dataset was created is important for evaluating its strengths and limitations. The core source is the Gallup World Poll, which has surveyed people in more than 150 countries using a standardized set of questions since 2005. Additional variables, including GDP per capita and healthy life expectancy, are drawn from organizations such as the World Bank, World Health Organization, and OECD. They're then used afterward through regression analysis to estimate how much each one correlates with why some countries score higher than others. The 2020 report reflects data averaged from 2017–2019 and was compiled by researchers at the Sustainable Development Solutions Network and Columbia University's Center for Sustainable Development, based on the original Gallup data.

Kayla Camejo — who funded the dataset

Regarding the creation of the dataset, the World Happiness Report is annually published by the Wellbeing Research Centre at the University of Oxford, an interdisciplinary research group focused on the study of human wellbeing that was founded in September 2019. They primarily worked alongside Gallup, a global analytics and advisory firm which was founded in 1935, and the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network (SDSN) that works under the UN Secretary-General to support action towards the UN Sustainable Development Goals.

It also draws on contributions from academic partners across North America, Europe, and East Asia, including University of British Columbia's Vancouver School of Economics, the Center for Sustainable Development at Columbia University, Simon Fraser University, Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, the International Society for Quality-of-Life Studies, and the Centre for Economic Performance based in Europe. Corporate sponsors vary year by year, mostly from consumer, health, and technology brands. It is important to acknowledge the possible biases that may arise in the data collection of over 140 countries without direct representation from many of these nations in the research process.

Yihan Liu — what the dataset can illuminate

One of the dataset's strengths is its consistency. Because the same survey methodology has been used across countries and over time, researchers can compare well-being both across nations and across years. This allows the dataset to illuminate patterns between happiness and factors such as health, income, social support, and governance. The horizontal comparison across countries and the vertical comparison across years could give hints to policymakers by connecting events and the reaction of people toward their life. The 2020 edition would be useful as it captures the "Happiness" trend before the COVID-19 pandemic, providing a useful baseline for later comparisons.

Jae Vin Lee — what information is left out

However, the dataset cannot reveal the full complexity of human well-being. By relying on national averages, it obscures the different experiences people may have within the same country. Happiness can vary greatly depending on income, race, ethnicity, gender, age, disability, religion, immigration status, sexuality, or the area of their residence. For example, a country may rank highly overall while some communities suffer poverty, discrimination, violence, unsafe housing, or limited healthcare access.

The data also leaves out many social, historical, and cultural factors that shape well-being. It does not include personal stories, local context, family relationships, community belonging, colonial history, war, political repression, racism, labor conditions, housing costs, climate change, or environmental injustice. In addition, some people may not be represented in the surveys due to homelessness, incarceration, displacement, language barriers, limited internet or phone access, or political instability.

Dan Pham & Vaishavi Kagita — the ideology of the dataset's ontology, and what would be missing if this were the only source

These omissions are not accidental but are connected to how the dataset is organized. By dividing happiness into categories of economic conditions, institutional quality, and individual well-being, the World Happiness Report treats happiness as something that can be quantified, compared, and explained through measurable indicators. This structure privileges forms of knowledge that can be counted while making other dimensions of well-being less visible. If this dataset were the only source available, much of the context behind the numbers would be lost because factors such as cultural values, spirituality, community belonging, and historical experiences are difficult to standardize across countries.

Dan Pham — the ideology of the dataset's ontology, continued

The organization of the spreadsheet itself reinforces this understanding. The first columns present a country's rank and happiness score, followed by explanatory variables such as GDP per capita, life expectancy, social support, freedom, generosity, and perceptions of corruption. This arrangement suggests that happiness can be understood and explained through these measurable indicators while encouraging readers to compare countries according to a common standard. By reducing entire countries to a single score, and placing them into rankings, the dataset obscures inequalities within countries and minimizes differences between groups, regions, and communities. The prominence of the ranking columns is especially significant because it frames happiness as something countries compete over rather than a complex social experience.

In this way, the ontology of the dataset presents happiness as a universal, measurable, and comparable phenomenon, shaping both what becomes visible in the data and how readers understand well-being itself.
