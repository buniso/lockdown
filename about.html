Processing

After obtaining the World Happiness Report 2020 dataset, we created a working copy of the spreadsheet to preserve the original data while allowing us to manipulate the dataset for analysis. As our third research question focused specifically on the apparent paradox of well-being within Sub-Saharan Africa, we filtered the dataset to include only countries classified within the Sub-Saharan Africa region. This intentional reduction of the dataset allowed us to conduct a focused case study rather than making broad global comparisons that fell outside our research scope.

We also removed variables that were not relevant to our analysis and retained only the columns needed to answer our research question, including country, GDP per capita, Ladder Score, Residual Happiness, and the six explanatory factors reported in the World Happiness Report (GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption). Simplifying the dataset made it easier to identify countries with similar levels of economic wealth but substantially different levels of reported well-being.

During processing, we compared GDP per capita values across countries to identify pairs with comparable economic conditions while examining differences in Ladder Scores and Residual Happiness. This process enabled us to isolate cases where economic wealth alone could not explain differences in well-being, supporting our investigation into the "African well-being paradox." This allowed us to discover and build the Ivory Coast and Zambia case study. Rather than treating the dataset as neutral, these processing decisions reflect the interpretive nature of Digital Humanities work: selecting, filtering, and organizing data are analytical choices that shape the scope, the questions researchers are able to ask, and the conclusions they can draw.

Presentation

That same interpretive logic carried over into how we chose to present the data. All of our visualizations, including the choropleth map, were built in Tableau, which let us work directly from the World Happiness Report dataset and iterate on a single consistent visual language across the site rather than stitching together charts from different tools. We moved the choropleth map earlier on the page so it orients readers with a regional overview before they encounter the closer Ivory Coast–Zambia comparison, and moved the timeline up to sit alongside that comparison, reinforcing the argument once readers already understand the stakes rather than introducing unfamiliar countries before any framing is given. This follows Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic's principle in Storytelling with Data that visualizations should be sequenced by what the audience needs to know at each point, not by the order in which we produced them.

Within Tableau, we matched each chart type to the specific question it needed to answer rather than defaulting to one format throughout. The GDP–happiness scatterplot uses a fitted prediction line so readers can see the overall trend and immediately spot outliers like Costa Rica and Afghanistan; the ranked gap chart uses diverging color (green for higher-than-predicted, red for lower) so readers can scan for the countries that depart most from GDP's prediction without reading every value; the faceted social-factor scatterplots let readers compare social support, freedom, and generosity side by side rather than crowding them into a single cluttered chart; and the dumbbell chart used for the Sub-Saharan African case studies was chosen specifically because it shows the gap between predicted and actual happiness as a visible distance, which suits our argument better than a bar chart would. In each case, we followed Knaflic's de-cluttering principles: removing gridlines and redundant legends, labeling outlier countries and key values directly on the chart, and limiting color to what carries meaning (for example, using color only to mark "above" versus "below" prediction).

We also enlarged charts and labels and increased text contrast so the visualizations remain legible for readers with low vision or on smaller screens, and we gave each chart a plain-language caption stating its main takeaway. Ultimately, though, no amount of careful chart choice or de-cluttering can resolve the deeper questions raised by the dataset itself — questions we turn to next.

One deliberate design choice worth naming here: the site borrows visual language from Animal Crossing: New Horizons; the rounded dialogue boxes, the warm island palette, the wobbly hand-drawn shapes. That game launched in March 2020, the same month lockdowns began, and became a phenomenon specifically because it sold a fantasy of leisure and "island happiness" during a period of real economic precarity. Tom Nook, its central figure, is also a well-known parody of capitalism: he mortgages your house and gates your progress behind currency, all wrapped in cheerful, cozy branding. Given this project's argument (that GDP measures something real but incomplete about happiness) I think it felt very fitting to match that in the design itself.
