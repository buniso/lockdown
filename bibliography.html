Reference

Annotated Bibliography

Chicago-style annotations, grouped by the team member who researched and wrote them.

Dan Pham

Greyling, Talita, Stephanie Rossouw, and Martijn J. Burger. 2026. "Cheerful Discontent: Understanding the African Well-Being Paradox." Social Indicators Research 182 (3). doi.org/10.1007/s11205-026-03842-8

This article argues that the gap between daily positive emotions and overall life satisfaction in Sub-Saharan Africa is driven by shared psychological factors interacting with local social and economic conditions rather than by fundamentally different concepts of happiness. The authors analyze Gallup World Poll data (2013–2024) and introduce a new positive affect–life evaluation (PA-LE) balance to compare emotional well-being and life satisfaction across regions. It informs our research by showing that social support, community belonging, and spirituality can sustain emotional well-being despite poverty, while limited opportunities reduce life satisfaction — a framework for why some Sub-Saharan African countries report higher or lower happiness than their economic conditions predict.

Schyns, Peggy. 1998. "Crossnational Differences in Happiness: Economic and Cultural Factors Explored." Social Indicators Research 43 (1/2): 3–26. doi.org/10.1023/a:1006814424293

Schyns argues that economic prosperity is a stronger predictor of happiness than culture, while also showing that people evaluate their lives through social comparisons rather than objective living conditions alone, using cross-national data from 40 countries and the Easterlin Paradox. It provides a useful theoretical framework for our research by explaining why countries with similar GDP per capita can report different levels of happiness.

Tov, William, and Ed Diener. 2009. Review of Culture and Subjective Well-Being. Social Indicators Research Series 38: 9–41. doi.org/10.1007/978-90-481-2352-0_2

Tov and Diener argue that subjective well-being is both universal and culture-specific: people everywhere experience positive emotions, negative emotions, and life satisfaction, but cultures differ in how these components are interpreted and combined. It cautions against treating happiness scores as directly comparable without considering cultural context, strengthening our research by suggesting that regional differences may reflect how people evaluate their lives, not just differences in economic prosperity.

Yihan Liu

Goff, Leonard, John F. Helliwell, and Guy Mayraz. 2018. "Inequality of Subjective Well-Being as a Comprehensive Measure of Inequality." Economic Inquiry 56 (4): 2177–94. doi.org/10.1111/ecin.12582

The article argues that income inequality (measured by the Gini coefficient) has limitations for understanding well-being, and that inequality in well-being should instead be measured more comprehensively across society. Using surveys across countries, the authors show the dispersion of happiness correlates more with average happiness and social trust than with income alone.

Pflug, Jan. 2008. "Folk Theories of Happiness: A Cross-Cultural Comparison of Conceptions of Happiness in Germany and South Africa." Social Indicators Research 92 (3): 551–63. doi.org/10.1007/s11205-008-9306-8

Pflug argues happiness is perceived differently across cultures, based on how 57 German and 44 South African students answered "What is happiness to you?" Individualism appears in German responses while collectivism appears in South African responses, providing further analysis of how ladder scores vary by region with different conceptions of happiness.

Ngoo, Yee Ting, Nai Peng Tey, and Eu Chye Tan. 2014. "Determinants of Life Satisfaction in Asia." Social Indicators Research 124 (1): 141–56. doi.org/10.1007/s11205-014-0772-x

The authors assert that Western-centered determinants of life satisfaction do not apply uniformly to Asia, using cross-national AsiaBarometer survey data across four regions to show marital status, standard of living, and governance are valued differently — emphasizing that even within Asia, life satisfaction is driven by different factors.

Vaishavi Kagita

Fors Connolly, Filip, Mikael Hjerm, and Tommy Garling. 2026. "Economic Development and Emotional Well-Being: Longitudinal Evidence from 33 European Countries." Scientific Reports 16: 20568. doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-59695-1

This article argues economic development is positively related to both life satisfaction and emotional well-being across European countries, using four waves of European Social Survey and World Bank GDP data. It helps explain how GDP relates to happiness while distinguishing life satisfaction from emotional well-being.

Mikucka, Malgorzata, Francesco Sarracino, and Joshua K. Dubrow. 2017. "When Does Economic Growth Improve Life Satisfaction? Multilevel Analysis of the Roles of Social Trust and Income Inequality in 46 Countries, 1981–2012." World Development 93: 447–459. doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2017.01.002

This article argues economic growth does not automatically increase life satisfaction — social trust and lower income inequality determine whether growth improves well-being, showing that GDP alone cannot fully explain differences in happiness between countries.

Portela, Marta, Isabel Neira, and Maria del Mar Salinas-Jimenez. 2013. "Social Capital and Subjective Wellbeing in Europe: A New Approach on Social Capital." Social Indicators Research 114 (2): 493–511. doi.org/10.1007/s11205-012-0158-x

This article argues social capital — trust, networks, community relationships — has a strong positive effect on subjective well-being in European countries, demonstrating that social relationships contribute to happiness beyond economic factors.

Jae Vin Lee

Diener, Ed, Weiting Ng, James Harter, and Raksha Arora. 2010. "Wealth and Happiness across the World: Material Prosperity Predicts Life Evaluation, Whereas Psychosocial Prosperity Predicts Positive Feeling." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 99 (1): 52–61. doi.org/10.1037/a0018066

Using Gallup World Poll data, the authors argue money and met basic needs are more connected to how people rate their overall life, while positive feelings are more connected to social support, respect, freedom, and meaningful activities — showing happiness is not a single thing. One weakness: the study is mostly correlational.

Aknin, Lara B., Christopher P. Barrington-Leigh, Elizabeth W. Dunn, John F. Helliwell, Justine Burns, Robert Biswas-Diener, Imelda Kemeza, Paul Nyende, Claire E. Ashton-James, and Michael I. Norton. 2013. "Prosocial Spending and Well-Being: Cross-Cultural Evidence for a Psychological Universal." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 104 (4): 635–652. doi.org/10.1037/a0031578

Using Gallup World Poll data from 136 countries plus experiments in Canada and Uganda, the authors find people who spend money on others report higher happiness, even controlling for income — relevant to the WHR's generosity factor. One caveat: giving money may carry different meanings across cultures.

Helliwell, John F., and Robert D. Putnam. 2004. "The Social Context of Well-Being." Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences 359 (1449): 1435–1446. doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2004.1522

The authors argue social capital — relationships with family, friends, neighbors, coworkers, and community — has a strong relationship with well-being and health, connecting to the WHR's social support variable. One weakness: it's difficult to know whether relationships cause happiness or happier people simply build more relationships.

Kayla Camejo

Gasiorowska, Agata, Kaja Glomb, Pelin Kesebir, and Richard Davidson. 2025. "Healthy Emotionality Protects from the Detrimental Well-Being Consequences of Low Economic Status." Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being 17 (4): e70059. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12363665

The authors argue healthy emotionality — the ability to experience and regulate emotion well — lets low-income individuals feel as much life satisfaction as higher-income individuals despite financial stressors, supporting our thesis that income is not the sole predictor of happiness.

Gebeyehu, T. K., R. Wells, M. Loomes, Z. Steel, and G. Kurt. 2026. "Social Support Coping Strategies among Sub-Saharan African Refugees: A Systematic Review and Meta-Synthesis." Cambridge Prisms: Global Mental Health 13: e43, 1–17.

This synthesis of 22 studies (463 participants) argues social support — family, friends, ethnic and community groups, cultural and religious supports — significantly helps Sub-Saharan African refugees cope with trauma, deepening our understanding of the WHR's "social support" category and its sub-themes.

Kotz, David M. 2002. "Globalization and Neoliberalism." Rethinking Marxism 12 (2): 64–79.

Kotz argues neoliberalism's dominance stems from the competitive structures of globalization rather than the policy's own positive effects, using U.S. economic data to trace GDP, investment, and export trends. It provides background on the forces shaping national GDP figures as we examine the presumed GDP–happiness relationship.

Xinyi Zhang

Rout, Heera Sahaya, Jishnu Sasi Kumar, and Bibhuti Bhusan Panda. 2020. "Understanding the Determinants of Happiness through Gallup World Poll." Indian Journal of Community Medicine 45 (4): 393–397. pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7652195

Using robust regression on Gallup World Poll data from 149 countries, the authors argue happiness is best explained by a combination of six covariates, with freedom and family the strongest predictors and trust showing no significant relationship — direct statistical support for our "happiness paradox" argument in lower-income and non-Western nations.

Proto, Eugenio, and Aldo Rustichini. 2013. "A Reassessment of the Relationship between GDP and Life Satisfaction." PLOS ONE 8 (11): e79358. doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0079358

Using five decades of World Values Survey data, the authors argue the GDP–life satisfaction relationship rises steeply among poor countries, flattens around $15,000–$17,000, and declines slightly among the richest nations — reconciling the Easterlin Paradox with cross-national studies and reinforcing that GDP is not a universal predictor.

Hu, Qingqing, Xiaobing Wang, and Mark Xu. 2021. "Are There Heterogeneous Impacts of Social Support on Subjective Well-Being?" National Accounting Review 3 (4): 360–376. doi.org/10.3934/NAR.2021019

Using a panel fixed-effects model across 53 countries, the authors argue social support's positive effect is significant in high-income countries but not in low-income ones, while generosity and freedom act as stronger moderators in low-income, low-generosity nations — complicating any single "social support = happiness" narrative.

Jasmine Gonzalez

Smith, Michael D., and Dennis Wesselbaum. 2023. "Well-Being and Income Across Space and Time: Evidence from One Million Households." Journal of Happiness Studies 24 (5): 1813–1840. doi.org/10.1007/s10902-023-00660-4

Using Gallup World Poll data spanning 158 countries and roughly 1.4 million people, the authors argue growth in income decreases inequality in well-being even where average levels stay flat over time — showing that once income inequality is controlled for, the Easterlin paradox largely disappears.

Behera, Deepak Kumar, Dil B. Rahut, M. Padmaja, and Ajit Kumar Dash. 2024. "Socioeconomic Determinants of Happiness: Empirical Evidence from Developed and Developing Countries." Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Economics 109: 102187. doi.org/10.1016/j.socec.2024.102187

Using quantile regression across 166 countries, the authors argue happiness in developing countries follows an inverted U-shaped pattern with income, and that social support and low corruption can outweigh raw income growth in shaping national happiness.

Abdur Rahman, Amanina, and Ruut Veenhoven. 2018. "Freedom and Happiness in Nations: A Research Synthesis." Applied Research in Quality of Life 13 (2): 435–456. doi.org/10.1007/s11482-017-9543-6

Synthesizing the World Database of Happiness, the authors argue economic, political, and personal freedom is linked to happiness with no "ceiling" effect even in the freest countries — helping explain why less wealthy but freer countries can still score well in the World Happiness Report.
