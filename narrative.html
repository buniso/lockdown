Narrative

What actually makes a country happy?

Introduction

— section lead: Yihan

When discussing people's satisfaction with their lives, we consider money, freedom, safety, employment status, etc. However, sometimes we never know what people's lives are like in different regions. There are constant discussions about certain areas' GDP and determining people's life satisfaction; without a doubt, it is superficial, but how do we know what makes us happy? To examine happiness more directly, the World Happiness Report provided sources for the project. This report researched citizens' responses to happiness by conducting a survey individually with the support from Gallop Wolrd Poll and other institutions. Each respondent ranked their happiness and satisfaction with their own life from worst (0) to best (10), which are called ladder scores. The research examines what the ladder scores capture, what affects them, and what is not represented. Wealth certainly captures and predicts happiness, but it does not fully explain it. Logged GDP per capita is one of the strongest metrics of a country's average ladder score, yet social support, freedom, and generosity account for much of the variation. For example, Sub-Saharan Africa, where scores diverge moderately from economic situations, places more weight on other metrics such as cultural difference and social connectedness. Our data comes from the World Happiness Report 2020, published by the UN Sustainable Development Network, which was sourced from the Gallup World Poll Surveys with the Cantril-ladder scores, along with other variables such as logged GDP per capita, social support, and healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption, etc. The data set provides a relatively subjective and standardized measure of a country's situation that can help us analyze what happiness entails and the limitations of the standardized variables.

Literature Review

— section lead: Xinyi

In recent discussions of global happiness, an issue has been whether economic prosperity alone can explain differences in national well-being. On the one hand, some scholars argue that income remains the strongest and most reliable predictor of happiness: Connolly, Hjerm, and Garling (2026) and Schyns (1998) both find that economic development is positively and consistently linked to life satisfaction across dozens of countries. From this perspective, wealth captures most of what matters: basic goods, opportunities, stability. On the other hand, however, others argue that this relationship weakens once basic needs are met, and that non-economic factors carry equal or greater explanatory power. Proto and Rustichini (2013), one of this view's main proponents, find that the GDP–happiness relationship "flattens and even declines" among the wealthiest nations, while Diener et al. (2010) show that material prosperity predicts life evaluation but fails to predict positive daily feeling, which is instead tied to social support, respect, and freedom. According to these sources, GDP is a necessary but insufficient variable. Helliwell and Putnam (2004), Portela, Neira, and Salinas-Jimenez (2013), and Aknin et al. (2013) all demonstrate that social capital, trust, and generosity independently predict well-being, while Hu, Wang, and Xu (2021) complicate even this claim by showing that social support's effect is significant only in high-income countries, meaning no single non-economic factor is universally reliable either. In sum, then, the issue is whether happiness is best explained by economic conditions or by the social and cultural fabric surrounding them. Sub-Saharan Africa sits at the center of this debate. Greyling, Rossouw, and Burger (2026) and Gebeyehu et al. (2026) attribute the region's persistent gap between predicted and reported happiness to community, spirituality, and family support systems. Yet Tov and Diener (2009) and Pflug (2008) caution that this divergence may partly reflect how happiness itself is culturally conceptualized rather than a true gap in well-being, raising doubts about whether standardized ladder scores measure an identical construct across regions. What remains underexplored, then, is how these competing economic, social, and cultural explanations interact simultaneously within a single standardized dataset like the World Happiness Report—particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, where the divide between economic prediction and lived happiness is most visible and least resolved.

Significance

— section lead: Xinyi

We are working on visualizing and analyzing the relationship between economic prosperity and happiness across the World Happiness Report 2020 dataset because we want to find out why countries with comparable GDP levels, particularly across Sub-Saharan Africa, report markedly different happiness scores than their economic conditions alone would predict, so that we can help others understand that national well-being cannot be reduced to a single standardized metric, but instead emerges from the interaction of economic, social, and cultural conditions specific to each region. While the literature offers strong theoretical arguments on both sides of the GDP debate, few studies bring these explanations together within a single dataset to map where and how far happiness departs from economic prediction, and fewer still isolate Sub-Saharan Africa as a region where this gap is visible. This project addresses that gap by comparing each country's actual ladder score against a GDP-based prediction, and then identifying countries that do not fit the expectation in terms of income for further exploration. It extends beyond the debate: international organizations and policymakers frequently rely on economic growth as a signal/shorthand for national progress, and when GDP is treated as sufficient evidence of a population's well-being, factors like social support, freedom, and culture are left out of policy. This project contributes to a more understandable representation of what actually improves people's lives, and cautions against relying on any single standardized measure to capture something as complex as well-being.

Research Question 1: How well does GDP predict happiness?

— section lead: Xinyi

The Common Assumption

When reviewing the six factors that the World Happiness Report contributes most to a person's ladder score, a natural inference to make is that wealth is the key to happiness given all of the resources it can afford someone. Higher economic output grants better healthcare, quality education, safe living conditions, and more personal autonomy. It allows nations to invest into their infrastructure and public programs, providing their citizens with secure environments and any assistance they may need. For these reasons, gross domestic product (GDP), the total monetary market value of all final goods and services made inside a country during a set time, is seen as a strong baseline for measuring human progress and satisfaction. To test this relationship, we created a scatterplot that compares the logged GDP per capita and ladder score of each country (Figure 1).

The Correlation

The scatterplot in Figure 1 provides strong support for this assumption. Each point represents a country, plotted according to its logged GDP per capita and its self-reported happiness score, or "ladder score," from the World Happiness Report. The dashed trend line reveals a clear positive relationship: as logged GDP per capita increases, happiness scores tend to rise as well. This relationship is further confirmed by the correlation coefficient of 0.78, indicating a strong positive association between economic prosperity and reported well-being. Wealthier nations, such as Finland and the United States, cluster consistently near the top of the happiness scale, reinforcing the idea that greater economic resources translate into higher levels of life satisfaction. Countries across nearly every world region follow this general upward trend, suggesting that the link between GDP and happiness holds fairly broadly rather than being limited to a specific part of the world.

The Outliers

Despite this strong overall trend, GDP alone does not fully account for the differences in happiness across countries, and several notable outliers illustrate the limits of its predictive power. Costa Rica, for example, reports a happiness score well above what its GDP per capita would predict, suggesting that factors beyond economic output, such as strong social support systems, life expectancy, or a sense of community, may play a significant role in its citizens' well-being. Conversely, Botswana falls below the trend line despite having a relatively higher GDP per capita, indicating that economic growth alone does not guarantee proportional gains in happiness. Afghanistan represents another important deviation, sitting near the bottom of both the GDP and happiness scales, which may reflect how factors such as political instability, conflict, and lack of basic security can suppress happiness independent of economic measures. These outliers demonstrate that while GDP correlates strongly with happiness on average, it is an incomplete predictor, and other social, political, and psychological factors must be considered to fully explain a country's level of well-being.

To understand why GDP falls short as an absolute predictor of well-being, we must look closer at the other factors that influence happiness and examine the impact they can make.

Beyond income, how do social factors help explain differences in happiness? The African Well-being Paradox

— section lead: Yihan, Dan

Wealth isn't the whole story. The World Happiness Report proposes several social determinants that may influence well-being beyond income. This section investigates whether different combinations of economic and social factors help explain regional differences in happiness, with particular attention to Sub-Saharan Africa.

Regional patterns reveal an important exception. Western Europe and North America cluster at higher levels of social support, freedom, and happiness, whereas Sub-Saharan African countries are more widely dispersed. Despite generally lower ladder scores, several countries in the region perform comparatively well on indicators such as freedom, suggesting that social factors may shape well-being differently than in other parts of the world. The following visualization explores this pattern by comparing how the major determinants contribute to predicted happiness across global regions.

This faceted bar chart compares the average contributions of five determinants — logged GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, and generosity — to predicted happiness across major world regions. Rather than displaying the raw values of these variables, the chart uses the World Happiness Report's "Explained by:" estimates, which quantify how much each factor contributes to regional ladder scores. By displaying each determinant in a separate panel, the visualization enables clear comparisons between regions without the clutter of grouped bar charts, making it easier to identify how different factors shape subjective well-being across geographic contexts.

Western Europe consistently shows the largest contributions from GDP per capita and healthy life expectancy, while Latin America and the Caribbean receive comparatively larger contributions from social support. Sub-Saharan Africa, however, presents a distinctive pattern: despite relatively smaller contributions from GDP per capita and healthy life expectancy, it exhibits comparatively larger contributions from generosity and freedom to make life choices. These patterns suggest that there is no single pathway to happiness. While material prosperity remains an important contributor to life evaluations, social and cultural resources also appear to play a meaningful role in shaping well-being. This finding supports Diener et al.'s distinction between material prosperity and psychosocial prosperity, suggesting that social relationships can contribute to happiness even where economic resources are more limited. These regional differences lay the foundation for the African Well-Being Paradox, explored in the following visualization through patterns of positive affect.

This treemap visualizes the distribution of positive affect across world regions, with each rectangle representing a region and its size corresponding to its overall positive affect score. Unlike life evaluations, which measure people's overall assessment of their lives, positive affect captures the frequency of everyday positive emotions such as enjoyment, laughter, and interest. The visualization reveals a striking pattern: Sub-Saharan Africa occupies the largest area, indicating the highest overall positive affect despite the region's comparatively low contributions from GDP per capita in the previous analysis.

This apparent contradiction reflects what Greyling, Rossouw, and Burger (2026) describe as the African Well-Being Paradox — the coexistence of high daily positive emotions alongside relatively low life evaluations. Rather than suggesting that Africans conceptualize happiness differently, the authors argue that this gap emerges because psychological and social resources interact with local economic conditions. While poverty and limited opportunities reduce people's evaluations of their lives, strong social support, community belonging, spirituality, and interpersonal relationships continue to sustain positive emotional experiences. This interpretation complements the previous visualization, which showed that Sub-Saharan Africa receives comparatively larger contributions from generosity and freedom to make life choices than would be expected from its level of economic prosperity. Together, these findings suggest that material prosperity and psychosocial prosperity represent distinct dimensions of well-being: income and health strongly influence how people evaluate their lives, whereas social connectedness and community engagement help sustain positive everyday experiences. The African Well-Being Paradox therefore demonstrates that social factors can explain important differences in happiness beyond what economic indicators alone are able to capture.

Overall, these findings answer the research question by demonstrating that social factors help explain differences in happiness beyond income. While economic prosperity remains important for overall life satisfaction, community belonging, social support, and strong interpersonal relationships can sustain positive emotional well-being even under conditions of material hardship, illustrating that happiness is influenced by both economic and social dimensions of life.

Why does Sub-Saharan Africa show some of the widest, and most debated, divergence between predicted and reported happiness? A Case Study on the Ivory Coast & Zambia.

— section lead: Dan

What is residual happiness?

While the previous visualizations demonstrate that wealth alone cannot fully explain global differences in happiness, Sub-Saharan Africa illustrates this pattern particularly clearly. Countries within the region often share similar levels of economic prosperity yet report markedly different levels of happiness, even after accounting for the key determinants included in the World Happiness Report.

To examine these differences, this analysis focuses on residual happiness rather than raw happiness scores. Residual happiness is the portion of a country's reported well-being that remains unexplained after accounting for six predictors: logged GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption.

The model estimates a country's expected happiness using these factors and Dystopia, a hypothetical country/baseline representing the lowest observed values for each predictor. The difference between this predicted summation and a country's actual reported happiness is its residual. Positive residuals indicate higher-than-expected happiness, while negative residuals indicate lower-than-expected happiness. Because residual happiness captures variation beyond what the model explains, it provides a useful lens for investigating the cultural, historical, and institutional influences on subjective well-being.

Residual Happiness Mapped: Divergence in Sub-Saharan Africa

To identify where these unexpected patterns occur, the following choropleth map visualizes residual happiness across the globe.

This choropleth map, created in Tableau extracting country-level data from the World Happiness Report 2020, uses colors and shades to represent Residual (+/-) values across regions. This type was chosen because it highlights geographic patterns and regional variation that are difficult to identify through summary statistics alone. By mapping these residuals rather than raw happiness scores, the visualization reveals where subjective well-being exceeds or falls short of expectations, highlighting regions that warrant closer investigation.

Countries shaded in blue report higher happiness than predicted, while countries shaded in orange report lower happiness than expected. Positive residuals appear across much of North America, Latin America, Western Europe, and Oceania, whereas several countries in South Asia, Eastern Europe, and parts of Southern Africa exhibit negative residuals. However, the map reveals that substantial geographic variation in happiness remains even after accounting for major determinants of well-being.

One notable finding is the heterogeneity within Sub-Saharan Africa: Niger (residual = +1.045) exhibits a strongly positive residual, while Botswana (residual = -1.715), Zambia, and Zimbabwe display strongly negative residuals. This contrast suggests that similar structural conditions do not necessarily produce similar experiences of well-being and points to the potential influence of unmeasured factors such as cultural values, community networks, and social cohesion. These patterns challenge simplified regional narratives and demonstrate the limitations of relying solely on quantitative indicators to explain happiness.

This map reflects Richard White's (2010) argument that spatial visualization is a method of research rather than simply a means of presenting findings. By mapping residual happiness instead of raw happiness scores, the visualization reveals unexpected patterns, such as the coexistence of strongly positive and strongly negative residuals within Sub-Saharan Africa that would be obscured by regional averages. As Presner and Shepard (2016) argue, geospatial analysis enables researchers to uncover spatial relationships and challenge dominant interpretations; in this case, the map complicates the assumption that countries with similar economic and social conditions will experience similar levels of well-being.

Case Study: Ivory Coast and Zambia

To better understand this divergence, this case study compares Ivory Coast and Zambia. Although the two countries have virtually identical levels of economic prosperity, with logged GDP per capita values of 8.226 and 8.225, respectively, they report significantly different levels of subjective well-being. Ivory Coast records a Ladder Score of 5.233 and a positive residual happiness score of +1.109, indicating that people report substantially higher well-being than predicted by the World Happiness Report model. In contrast, Zambia records a Ladder Score of 3.759 and a negative residual of -0.838, suggesting that reported well-being falls considerably below model predictions. By examining their contrasting outcomes, this analysis explores how social, cultural, historical, and institutional factors may shape subjective well-being beyond what economic indicators alone can explain.

Historical context provides one possible explanation for this divergence. As illustrated in the accompanying TimelineJS above, Ivory Coast and Zambia followed distinctly different political and social trajectories in the decade preceding the 2020 World Happiness Report. Schyns (1998) argues that individuals evaluate their lives through social comparison rather than objective living conditions alone. From this perspective, Ivory Coast's transition from political conflict to economic recovery, increasing stability, and the resurgence of community-based support networks such as tontines, which are informal mutual aid associations that reinforce trust and reciprocity, may have fostered a sense of collective progress, leading people to evaluate their lives more positively than economic indicators alone would predict.

Zambia, by contrast, experienced political instability alongside prolonged droughts that disrupted agricultural livelihoods and strained community support systems. Rather than reinforcing perceptions of recovery, these cumulative pressures likely contributed to lower life evaluations despite comparable levels of economic prosperity. As Tov and Diener (2009) argue, subjective well-being is shaped not only by universal human needs but also by the historical and cultural contexts through which people interpret their experiences.

Although these contrasting trajectories are not directly captured by the World Happiness Report's six explanatory variables, they help explain why two countries with nearly identical logged GDP per capita entered the 2020 survey with juxtaposing residual happiness scores.

In conclusion, the comparison suggests that Sub-Saharan Africa exhibits some of the widest divergence between predicted and reported happiness because countries with similar economic conditions often possess profoundly different historical trajectories, social institutions, and community relationships. The contrasting experiences of Ivory Coast and Zambia demonstrate that residual happiness captures meaningful aspects of subjective well-being that extend beyond GDP, health, freedom, and other conventional indicators. Rather than representing unexplained error, these residuals reveal the influence of historical recovery, institutional trust, and social cohesion on how people evaluate their lives.

Conclusion

Across all these research questions, the same pattern kept emerging: GDP is a strong predictor of happiness, but it is not the full picture. Countries like Costa Rica, Finland, and Niger all report higher happiness than their GDP alone would predict, while countries like Afghanistan and Botswana fall below what their economic numbers suggest. Our case study on Ivory Coast and Zambia made this especially clear — two countries with nearly identical GDP per capita but very different outcomes, shaped less by economics and more by political stability, community networks, and historical context. Together, these findings support our thesis: money can, to some extent, buy happiness, but it cannot fully explain it. As our data critique makes clear, no single standardized number can capture everything that it means for a country's people to live well.
